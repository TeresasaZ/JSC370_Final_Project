Teresa Zhao

Project Description

Introduction

Urban traffic congestion is a key urban planning challenge. This project investigates which factors influence traffic volume in Toronto, using data from 2018–2022. While my midterm focused on fuel prices alone, this final project expands the analysis to explore additional variables such as time of day, weekday/weekend patterns, and the COVID-19 pandemic period.

Research Question

What are the key factors that influence traffic volume in Toronto?

Data Sources

Traffic Volume: Toronto Open Data (15-minute intervals)

Fuel Prices: Open Canada (monthly taxed fuel prices in Toronto)

Interactive Plots

Figure 6: Interactive plot comparing hourly traffic volume patterns on weekdays versus weekends

Figure 6 specifically compares hourly traffic volume patterns between weekdays and weekends. The plot highlights a clearer trend: on weekdays, there are two distinct peaks around 8 AM and 5 PM, corresponding to morning and evening commutes. In contrast, weekend traffic is more evenly distributed, with higher volumes around midnight and a single peak around noon.

Figure 8: Interactive Leaflet map of average traffic volume by location in Toronto (2018–2022)

Figure 8 displays traffic volumes by location, where larger circles represent higher average volumes. The map reveals that downtown Toronto and major routes—such as the Don Valley Parkway—experience significantly higher traffic, while suburban areas like Scarborough generally show lower average volumes.

Figure 9: 3D scatter plot showing the relationship between hour of day, fuel price, and 15-minute traffic volume (sample of 1,000 observations)

Figure 9 explores the relationship between traffic volume, fuel price, and hour of day using a 3D scatter plot. To keep the visualization concise and interpretable, a random sample of 1,000 observations was selected from the full dataset. By interacting with the 3D plot, it becomes evident that traffic volume does not follow a clear pattern with fuel price. In contrast, volume varies substantially with the hour of day—a trend consistent with earlier findings in Figure 6, where distinct peaks were observed during commuting hours.
